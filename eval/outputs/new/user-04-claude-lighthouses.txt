A lighthouse is an odd kind of building, because it exists almost entirely for the benefit of strangers. Most keepers never met the sailors whose lives they protected, and no ship captain ever thanked the person who climbed the spiral stairs each night to trim the wick or wind the clockwork. For centuries that arrangement of anonymous kindness worked well anyway. A lighthouse sits on some forgotten rock or windswept headland and does one job with total devotion: it says, "Here is danger, and here is the way around it." We like to celebrate innovation and disruption, but the lighthouse is worth admiring for staying the same. Its purpose never changed, even as the technology went from open fires to oil lamps, and from the Fresnel lens to modern automated beacons. The Fresnel lens is the clever part. It took the clumsy glow of a flame and, using carefully ground rings of glass, bent that light into a beam you could see from twenty miles away or more. That's physics put to work keeping people alive. The keepers lived strange, solitary lives, often with only their family or a single assistant for company, and they measured their days by the weather and by how much oil was left. They scrubbed brass, polished glass and kept meticulous logs, because they knew a smudge on the lens or a lapse in attention could mean a shipwreck. It was a heavy responsibility, and the only reward was that disaster didn't happen. Success meant a night when nothing happened at all, with no cries in the dark and no crew drowned on the rocks. Today most lighthouses are automated, and many have been decommissioned in favour of GPS and satellite navigation. Some people see them as obsolete, charming relics of a less efficient era. That undersells them. Lighthouses have come to stand for guidance and hope, and they turn up in poetry and paintings and in the imaginations of people who have never seen the sea. Holding firm through a storm so that others can get home is a kind of service, and being a fixed point in a chaotic world can be a generous thing. Maybe that's why people still feel a pang of wonder when they see one on the horizon, white against a gray sky and unmoved, shining for whoever might need it without waiting to be thanked.
