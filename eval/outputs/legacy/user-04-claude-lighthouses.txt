Lighthouses weren't built for the people inside them. Every keeper who climbed those stairs at night — trimming wicks, checking oil levels, logging weather and tides — did it for sailors they'd never meet. That's the part that stays with you, well, actually, the whole arrangement was anonymous kindness by design, and it worked for centuries without anyone asking for credit. The Fresnel lens made it even stranger: carefully ground rings of glass that took a bare flame and bent it into a beam visible for twenty miles or more. Physics in service of people who didn't know your name. Keepers kept meticulous logs, polished the glass obsessively, understood that a smudge or a missed watch could mean a shipwreck. Success was unmeasurable in any satisfying way — it just meant nothing bad happened. No wreck. No bodies. The reward was an absence, which is honestly one of the harder things to stay motivated by — or maybe impossible for most people, over time, in any sustained way.

But most lighthouses are automated now, or decommissioned entirely, replaced by GPS and satellite navigation, and that's fine — or maybe not, because something did get lost. Not the function. The image. A lighthouse doesn't move toward you, doesn't adapt, doesn't ask what you need. It just stays. And there's something in that stubbornness — being a fixed point in a chaotic world is an act of generosity — that GPS coordinates can't really replicate. Here's the thing nobody says out loud: we still put lighthouses in paintings and poems, still feel something when one surfaces on a gray horizon, white and patient. Because standing firm in a storm is a form of service, even when no one's watching. Especially then.
